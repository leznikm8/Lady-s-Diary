# Lady-s-Diary
Two hundred problems from an almanac for women

In 1709 a woman called Mary Wright sent an answer to a puzzle about the shadow of a church steeple. She won the prize. She had also changed one of the numbers on purpose, so that nobody could win by simply measuring the real steeple. That was the kind of reader The Ladies' Diary had.

I decided to put my Claude account to work on something that might be useful to everyone. After some thought, I chose The Ladies' Diary, an English almanack that ran from 1704 to 1840 and was famous for its mathematical problems. The problems survive. You can find the old volumes as scans online, and some OCR has been done on them. But in practice they are almost unreadable. The scans are blurred, the OCR is full of errors, the notation is two centuries out of date, and the questions are written in the English of the 1700s, often in verse. The mathematics is still good. It has just been locked away.

A magazine for women that printed calculus

John Tipper, a schoolmaster in Coventry, launched the Diary in 1704. It was a pocket almanack for women, with a calendar, recipes and useful tables. It also had a page of riddles and mathematical questions, answered by readers the following year. Within a few years, the mathematics had taken over.

Women set the questions as well as answering them. Barbara Sidway asked for the largest cylinder that fits inside a cone, which is a calculus problem, in the first decade of the century. Lydia Fisher, Sarah Brown and Mary Nelson set questions too. Later came the men who became famous: Thomas Simpson, a weaver's son who became professor of mathematics at Woolwich, and who signed himself by comic pseudonyms; William Emerson; John Landen, a land agent from Peterborough; and Charles Hutton, who later edited the Diary himself. It asked for no degree. It printed the best answer.

In 1817 Thomas Leybourn collected every question and answer into four volumes. Those volumes are the source for this project.

What we made

Working with Claude, I went through the two volumes I could get hold of, covering 1704–1748 and 1802–1816, and produced three booklets:

Book One: 102 maths problems from 1707–1748, sorted by age from 5 to 17, following the English school stages.
Book Two: 57 maths problems from 1802–1816.
Beyond A-level: 34 harder problems from both volumes, including the mechanics: pendulums, hanging chains, rolling balls and a falling Moon.

Every problem is retold in modern English, with old money and measures converted where it matters, and every answer has been recomputed. Along the way we found that the Diary was sometimes wrong, and each booklet has a small box saying so. One solver claimed a fares problem had "innumerable answers"; it has exactly eight. Another printed a pendulum length to twenty decimal places; the last four are wrong. Where two solvers disagreed, we say who was right.

Claude drafted the solutions and retelling, recomputed every answer, ran OCR on the scans and typeset everything in LaTeX. I chose the problems, made the editorial decisions and checked the result. Each booklet says so.

Where to find it

A magazine for women published advanced math problems for 136 years. The least we can do is make them readable again.
